What Is The Patoshi Pattern and What Does It Have to Do With Bitcoin Inventor Satoshi Nakamoto?

In late May 2020, a Bitcoin wallet that had been dormant for 11 years came back to life and sent its 50 BTC balance to two different addresses. This sent shockwaves throughout the entire cryptocurrency industry as many speculated that these coins could actually belong to Satoshi Nakamoto himself.

In late May 2020, a Bitcoin wallet that had been dormant for 11 years came back to life and sent its 50 BTC balance to two different addresses. This sent shockwaves throughout the entire cryptocurrency industry as many speculated that these coins could actually belong to Satoshi Nakamoto himself.

For those unaware, Satoshi is the anonymous creator of the network and his/her/their identity has never been revealed. He disappeared from the scene back in 2011 and nobody has heard from him since.

Well, the previously dormant wallet contained coins that were mined in February 2009 - just one month after the Bitcoin protocol itself went live. This meant that whoever had mined these coins must have been interested in Bitcoin in the very earliest days, when Bitcoin was still completely obscure. 

However, although we may never conclusively know whether it was Satoshi or someone else that moved those coins, there is some evidence to suggest that it probably wasn’t Satoshi.

The most commonly referenced evidence pointing towards this conclusion is known as the "Patoshi" pattern.

The Patoshi pattern

The Patoshi pattern refers a blockchain analysis technique that tracks the ExtraNonce fields in the coinbase (no, we're not talking about the popular crypto exchange) field of the coinbase transaction that creates Bitcoin. The method was introduced by RSK Labs chief scientist Sergio Demian Lerner, a researcher and top-level cryptographer in his own right.

He released a study in 2013 that suggested that the vast majority of the initial BTC mined was done by just one single miner. In this original paper, he provided evidence that suggested a single miner could have possibly processed 22,000 blocks in the first year of Bitcoin. Lerner estimated that the miner was able to gather at least 1.8 million Bitcoin over the period, of which 1.1 million Bitcoin has still not even been spent. 

Lerner dubbed this miner as "Patoshi".

The analyst found three privacy-related flaws that enabled him to correlate the blocks mined during the first year and uncover a common origin through the original Satoshi client v0.1 source code.

His research allowed him to compute the mining hash rate of each of the early miners and he went on to link the different blocks to the same miner and found a specific pattern for one of the miners who was mining a whole lot of Bitcoin. 

This was because the hash rate was at a very low level throughout 2009 and it remained consistent throughout the period. Interestingly enough, this hash rate was also consistent with the hash rate that was present in the first 14 days of Bitcoin in which Satoshi himself was likely to be the only miner.

Here's a graph visualizing the Patoshi pattern

This is the Patoshi Pattern. Each blue line is the miner that has been dubbed as Patoshi with all the other green lines being other different miners. 

The following image is the original visualization that Lerner released;

The black lines actually represent the ExtraNonce increase over time as a series of slopes. The black slopes appear to be of a similar gradient and they never overlap or go to zero once they reach a certain height. This is the evidence that Lener used to suggest that they all belonged to one miner that mined over 1 million BTC.

New evidence

He went on to release another paper in April 2019 to debunk a lot of the criticism related to his first paper. The second paper was called “The Return of the Deniers and the Revenge of Patoshi” in which he provided new evidence to back up the original Patoshi pattern. The new evidence was stated to provide an overwhelming probability that a single miner had mined all the coins in his Pathoshi pattern.

The new evidence was based on computer clocks as miners would timestamp blocks with their local computer clock after processing them. His evidence suggests that there were no time inversions between the Patoshi blocks which suggests that one PC clock was responsible for timestamping almost 43% of the blocks in the first 50,000 blocks. This had to be Satoshi himself. There was even a time when Patoshi turned his computer off for a period of 10 days, which produced the following result:

The gap where the blue lines stop is where Patoshi stopped mining for a few days. The other green dots are other miners again.

So, why was the 11 year old wallet not linked to Patoshi (or Satoshi)?

Well, Twitter user Nic Carter pointed out that the specific block that these coins were generated from (Block number 3654) was not actually part of this specific Patoshi pattern.

So from the research suggested by Lerner, the block that mined these BTC was not actually mined by this dominant miner dubbed as Patoshi and, therefore, is most likely not Satoshi Nakamoto.

Furthermore, it just doesn’t make sense for Satoshi to come back and spend coins from some random block. As pointed out by Adam Back - if Satoshi wanted to sell some coins, he would be selling his most recently mined coins first, and not coins that were mined in the very earliest days of Bitcoin.
